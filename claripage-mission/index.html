Why we built it

Our child was studying hard and doing badly. It took us far too long to work out why.

ClariPage started at a kitchen table, with a pile of science notes that were missing more than they contained.

The problem wasn't studying

Our child was working. Sitting down, going over it, doing the thing we asked. And still coming home with marks that didn't match the effort.

When we finally looked properly at what he was revising from, the answer was obvious and slightly heartbreaking. He was studying a page that was missing a third of the lesson. He'd been revising his own gaps, faithfully, for weeks.

No amount of trying harder fixes that.

Why existing tools didn't help

Flashcard apps assume the notes are already good, and make a parent type every term in by hand. Homework-answer apps give a child the answer and teach them nothing. General AI assistants will produce a confident page of text with no way to tell which parts came from the teacher and which the machine invented.

All three share an assumption: that the page in front of the child is complete. In primary and middle school, it almost never is. Taking notes is a skill children are still learning, and we grade them as though they've already learned it.

So we built the thing that shows the gaps

ClariPage fills in what a page is missing. Plenty of tools will do that much.

What's different is that it labels every point with where it came from. What your child wrote. What came off a handout. What had to be added. What was wrong and got corrected.

Keeping those separate is the whole product. It's also the harder engineering choice, and we'd make it again.

What that gives a parent

You can see the gap without understanding the subject. You don't need to remember how to factor a quadratic. You need to see that half the page is labelled "added".

You find out in October, not at the report card. A missed concept is cheap to fix in the week it happens and expensive to fix a term later.

You can tell effort apart from understanding. The practice test gives two scores. A child strong on their own notes and weak on the additions doesn't need to work harder. They need to write more down.

You can see what the AI said. Not a wall of confident text. A labelled page you can check.

What we believe

Evidence first. A study tool should show its sources. If we can't tell you where something came from, we shouldn't be asking your child to memorise it.

Better notes, not fewer notes. The goal is a child who takes better notes next term, not one who stops bothering because an app will cover for them. So we name what was missed, every single time. The note-taking habit is worth more in the long run than any set of flashcards.

Studying should never be rationed. Capturing a page costs us money and is limited. Practising, reviewing and testing aren't, and won't be. An app that charges a child to keep studying has its incentives pointed the wrong way.

Write for the child in front of you. A page for a seven-year-old shouldn't read like a page for a sixteen-year-old. Choose the reading level and everything follows it: the notes, the questions, even how busy the diagrams are.

The adult holds the account. Children study on a parent's or teacher's login and never hand over their own details. That was a design decision before it was a legal one.

It should refuse things. It won't answer the worksheet. It won't work on anything that isn't schoolwork. A tool used by children should have things it declines to do.

Say plainly what it can't do. ClariPage reads handwriting well and still sometimes gets it wrong. It draws diagrams that teach and occasionally draws one that's off. We show a confidence rating and label every addition, because a child who checks is better off than a child who trusts.

Who it's for

Parents of children roughly six to fourteen, mostly. The years where note-taking is being learned rather than assumed, where a parent can still help but is starting to lose track of the material, and where a small gap left alone becomes a large one.

Also teachers turning a handout into practice, and older students whose own notes need rescuing.

ClariPage is in testing and not yet publicly available.
