The Tuesday night problem

The test is Thursday. You ask to see their notes.

Half a page of handwriting. Three bullet points that trail off mid-sentence. A diagram you can't identify. A formula that might be wrong, and you're not sure enough to correct it.

You don't know what the teacher actually said. Your child doesn't remember. So you guess, or you re-teach a topic you last saw twenty-five years ago, or you say "go over it again" and hope.

What's actually worrying you

Children don't fall behind in a week. They fall behind one missed concept at a time. Fractions in fourth grade quietly become algebra in eighth.

By the time a report card tells you, the gap is months old. And when your child studies hard and still does badly, nobody can tell you why. Was it effort? Understanding? Or notes that were missing the exact thing the test asked about?

That last one is far more common than parents realise, and it is the only one that never shows up in a grade.

ClariPage reads the page and shows its working

Photograph your child's notes. You get back organized study notes — and every single point is labelled with where it came from.

your notesWhat your child actually wrote. Tidied up, meaning unchanged.
from the handoutText that came off a printed page rather than their handwriting.
addedNot on the page at all. Filled in because the topic needs it.
fixedThe page said something factually wrong. This is the correction.
referenceDrawn from study material you added yourself.

Count the yellow. That is how much of Tuesday's lesson never made it onto the page.

You didn't have to sit in the classroom, and you didn't have to understand the topic yourself. You can see the shape of what's missing in about ten seconds.

Then it tells you which problem you have

Every set of notes ends with a plain summary of what was left out. And after a practice test, ClariPage gives you two scores rather than one: how your child did on material they wrote down, and how they did on material their notes had missed.

Those two numbers answer the question you've never been able to answer.

  • Strong on their own notes, weak on the additions — they understand the lesson, but they aren't capturing it. That's a note-taking problem, and it's fixable in a fortnight.
  • Weak on both — the concept didn't land. Now you know to go back to it, and which part.
  • Strong on both — they're fine. Stop worrying and let them go and play.

This is the thing you cannot get from a grade, from a teacher with twenty-eight other children, or from asking a nine-year-old how school went.

It will not do their homework

We think this is why most parents hesitate over an AI study app, and they're right to.

ClariPage organizes what was taught and names what's missing. It will not answer the worksheet. Ask it to work on anything that isn't schoolwork and it declines.

And because every added line carries a label, you never have to wonder what a machine told your child. You can see it. That's the difference between a study tool and a chatbot your child uses in a closed room.

So they can study without you standing over them

Notes are written at your child's reading level — a seven-year-old's page doesn't read like a fourteen-year-old's. Everything can be read aloud, which for younger children is the difference between working alone and needing an adult beside them.

Then there are flashcards, a matching game, typed recall practice, a scored practice test, and a review queue that brings cards back just before they'd be forgotten. A third-grader can work through a round of cards while you make dinner.

Studying is never rationed. Photographing pages has a monthly limit, because processing them costs us money. Practising, reviewing and testing don't, and won't. Charging a child for the act of studying gets the whole thing backwards.

What you'll be able to say at the next conference

Not "I think he's struggling." Instead: "He understands the material. His notes are missing the worked examples, every time. Can he get a copy of those?"

That is a completely different conversation, and teachers can act on it.
