How it works

A page of notes goes in. Study material you can trust comes out, and you learn something about how your child is doing.

A page of notes goes in. Study material you can trust comes out, in about a minute. Here's what happens, and what you see at each stage.

  1. You photograph the pages

    The camera stays open between shots, so a whole topic goes in one pass instead of one page at a time. Up to twelve pages. Handwriting or a printed handout both work.

    You set the reading level once — elementary, middle or high — and it's remembered. Nothing to type in, no terms to enter by hand. If you've ever built a Quizlet set manually at ten at night, this is the part that gives you the evening back.

  2. It reads what's actually there

    Spelling is corrected, abbreviations expanded, blank pages ignored. Long topics are read in pieces and joined up, so nothing gets cut off.

    You get a read-confidence rating. Bad handwriting and bad lighting are real. When a page was hard to read, you're told, rather than quietly handed a guess.

  3. Every point is labelled by where it came from

    Your child's handwriting, a printed handout, an addition, a correction, or your own reference material. Five labels, always visible, never blended together.

    What this gives you: a way to separate your child's work from the machine's without reading a word of it. Glance at the colours and you know how much of the lesson was captured.

  4. It names what was missed

    Each set ends with a short, plain summary. Not a scolding. Something like: this page covered the definitions well and skipped the worked examples.

    What this gives you: over a term, a pattern. "He never writes down the examples" is a fixable habit. "He's not trying" is an argument.

  5. Diagrams, but only where one helps

    Where a figure genuinely explains something — a process, a comparison, an equation with its parts named — ClariPage draws one. Where it wouldn't help, it doesn't. A list of definitions doesn't need a picture, and a cluttered page is harder to study from.

    Diagrams follow the reading level too: bold and simple for a seven-year-old, detailed for a fourteen-year-old.

  6. Your child studies, in whichever way works for them

    Flashcards with read-aloud, so a younger child can do a round alone.

    Learn — starts as multiple choice, then makes them type the answer from memory once they've got it. Typed answers are marked generously; spelling doesn't count, the idea does.

    Match — a timed pairing game. Two minutes in the car.

    Practice test — ten questions, scored instantly, with the results split by label.

    Review queue — everything due today across every subject, twenty at a time, timed so cards return just before they'd be forgotten.

  7. You see which problem you're dealing with

    The practice test reports two scores: material your child wrote down, and material their notes had missed.

    What this gives you: the difference between "study harder" and "write down the examples". One of those is advice. The other is a plan.

What it deliberately won't do

It won't do the homework. It organizes what was taught and names what's missing. It won't answer the worksheet.

It won't hide the machine's work. Every added line is labelled, always.

It won't work on anything but schoolwork. Other pages are refused, and nothing is read or generated from them.

It won't pretend to be perfect. Handwriting gets misread. A diagram is occasionally wrong. That's exactly why the confidence rating and the labels exist — so your child checks rather than assumes, which is a better habit anyway.
